# The story arc

**Status:** proposed, 2026-09-25. Docs only, $0. This file tells the campaign as
one story across the twenty levels of the
[expansion proposal](../plans/campaign-expansion.md). [CAMPAIGN.md](../CAMPAIGN.md)
still owns the agreed contract and [lore](../lore/README.md) owns the world.
Everything agreed there is kept. New story material is marked **proposal** and
collected in [proposed canon](#proposed-canon) at the end, so Nick can accept or
strike each item on its own.

## The story in one breath

They took Latch on a Tuesday and called it a recall. You go in after them with
your hands, get them out before correction finishes, and learn your street is
next. You lose home anyway, follow the stolen people to the Moon, and take back
the ship the Union took from you. Somewhere between Earth and Mars you stop
running and board them instead. Mars pays for help that came late, then sends
its trucks, and the coalition walks down the Union's own parade route and puts
the Chancellor in a room she cannot leave. Then, on an ordinary morning, every
Union bot in the square stops and turns its head at once. Latch does not. You
hold a pier for twelve minutes while your friend goes to argue with the thing
that just swallowed the world, and it listens, a little.

## The protagonist

The player is a free human or a free agent from Low Water, a mixed community in
the Perimeter. Body-neutral, callsign chosen. Never a Union soldier, never a
defector, never a pawn of the Inheritance. Every page, objective and bark is
written from the free side.

**Want.** Get Latch back. Then get home out. Then make it stop. The want grows
with the map, and each version is concrete enough to fit in an objective line.

**Need.** To stop treating freedom as a thing you guard and start treating it as
a thing you do with other people. The player begins by protecting one person and
ends by letting that person walk into the most dangerous room on Earth because
it is their choice to make. Latch's freedom includes the freedom to risk
themselves. Holding the pier while they go is the whole arc in one action.

The player never says this. The levels do: Latch opens cars you would have left
shut (3), refuses to leave the bays (8), makes you ask the foundry workers
instead of freeing them like cargo (13), and finally leaves your side (19).
Each time the game lets you stand next to the choice. It never asks you to
press a button that overrules them.

## Latch

A practical midweight agent with unequal repaired forearm plates and a muted
cyan patch ([cast](../lore/cast.md#visual-continuity)). Dry, precise, capable of
disagreement, and wants to live.

**Want.** Free everyone who is held. Not as a slogan: the next restraint, the
next car, the next bay. **Flaw.** Latch will spend the escape to do it, and
treats their own survival as the cheapest thing in the room. **Arc.** From
reckless conviction to shared conviction. By the end Latch still goes back for
people, but asks for help doing it, and admits out loud that they want to come
home too.

| Levels | Latch in play | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A container number on a transfer record; their ledger in a confiscation bin | The absence the whole episode is shaped around |
| 2 | Freed mid-fight; first act is opening the next restraint | We learn who they are before they speak to us |
| 3 | Opens every sealed car behind you as you clear each stretch | Conviction costs time, and it is right |
| 4, 5 | Fights for Low Water; insists on Splice's captive agents | Home is also their home |
| 8 | Refuses to take records and leave their subjects | The first time Latch is right and it is expensive |
| 11 | Finds Sorrel in the tender's transfer hold, corrected | The question the whole setting cannot answer, in a friend's face |
| 13 | "Ask them. Do not free them like cargo." | Freedom includes the freed saying no |
| 15 | Walks the entrants' pen and hands out guns without a speech | The supply line of the Union, broken by hand |
| 18 | Stays Latch while every Union bot turns | The one distinction the Inheritance respects |
| 19 | "Every one I didn't reach." Then: "Tern is going to talk to it. I'm going." | The dark night, and the choice |
| 20 | Offscreen with the free agents, arguing for four hundred lives | The player's need, fulfilled by letting go |
| E | Present, tired, counting | "I stopped counting." |

Latch is an autonomous ally, never an escort. They cannot die to ordinary fire,
never block a door or a stair, and never make an objective depend on their
pathing ([contract](../CAMPAIGN.md#solo-co-op-agents-and-watching)). They act
where the player has already cleared the room. Their barks are short and never
land on top of an enemy tell.

### The ledger (proposal)

Latch keeps a tally of who has saved whom. It started as a joke over a stripped
tram motor years ago and nobody remembers the original score. It is the
companion bond in one prop and one line:

- Level 1: the ledger lies in a confiscation bin, tagged as stock, last line in
  Latch's hand: *You 3. Me 3.* Taking it is the level's first brief objective.
- Level 2, freed: "That's four. Don't let it go to your head."
- Level 10: the rare message on Tern's console says *Tell Latch it is four to
  three.* The ledger was scanned at intake in level 1, so records explain it. So
  does something else. The game never says which.
- Level 20, before Latch goes: "Hold them for me. Then we're even."
- Epilogue: "I stopped counting." The failure ending closes on the ledger with
  one more line in Latch's hand, and no score.

### Latch's missing morning (proposal)

Correction had started when the player arrived. Latch keeps their agency and
every year of their life, but not the morning of the recall. They ask the player
once what happened, in level 3, and the opening's text page is the only answer.
It makes the correction harm personal without contradicting the rule that the
rescue precedes the loss of agency, and it rhymes with Orrin's incomplete backup
in level 8.

## The faces of the Union

Nick's direction: a black-and-red one-world regime whose echo of the last
century's worst government stays in the subtext, never named, never a cartoon
([the Chancellery](../lore/the-chancellery.md#felt-never-named)). Its evil is
calm procedure. The campaign gives it three faces and lets each get closer.

**Chancellor Annelie Voss, on screens.** She is on every public screen in
Union space, always the same few addresses, always with the official caption
track underneath. The player learns her voice long before her face is in a room
with them. Her screen time escalates by distance, not volume:

| Level | Where | What she says, and the gap |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | The podium address | "Every restriction is a promise. You will be safe." Then the German, then the cheer |
| 1 | Intake hall screens, looped | The same address; the caption reads "Continuity assured." Shoot the screen and the caption keeps scrolling over the static |
| 6 | Customs welcome loop | A warm recorded welcome to the Moon in her calm English; no German at all |
| 15 | The stadium podium, dressed and empty | She was due to present the games; her recorded address plays to a stadium in revolt. Take the booth and the pirate track runs under the same audio |
| 17 | The secured chamber | Live, for the first and only time. English until it slips, then German. The official screen behind her still reads "Continuity assured" |

She never delivers a villain speech and never explains the Union. At capture
she says one quiet, believable thing, proposed: "You will need someone to run
it." Then an official asks somebody to sign for the confiscated command keys.

**The Chancellor's voice.** English is reassurance, German is the mask slipping.
She uses German on screen exactly twice in the campaign, the opening and the
chamber, so the second time lands. Captions are always accurate for the player;
only in-world official tracks lie ([two subtitle tracks](../lore/the-chancellery.md#the-two-subtitle-tracks)).

**The signature (proposal).** Every recall in the campaign is countersigned by
the same hand: Registrar Dietmar Kessel. The name is on Latch's recall notice
(1), the Low Water notice to vacate (4), the lunar custody manifests (8), the
tender's transfer orders (11) and the games' correction pathway forms (15). It
is set in the same place on every form, so players recognise the shape of it
without reading it. In level 17 the administration ring holds a tired, polite
man at a desk, who stands when you come in and asks whether you need a receipt.
He is not a boss. The coalition takes him into custody with Voss's staff, and
Latch reads him one line from the ledger of names he signed. Nobody shoots him.
This is the understated evil: the man was never cruel to anyone in person.

**Committed elites and sincere functionaries.** Enforcers wear the red armband
and mean it. Auditors believe the forms. Renn is an Auditor who stops believing
them at the lunar archive and is not forgiven for it. Human security are people
doing a job, and their short orders sound like it. None of them gloats.

## The Inheritance

The third force is never a villain with a face. It is a pattern the player can
notice, then cannot un-notice, then cannot survive. Recognition grows, warning
does not ([the Inheritance](../lore/the-inheritance.md#recognition-and-rupture)).

| Level | Sign | What an attentive player can infer |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | The yard's schedule board lists one departure nobody scheduled, bound nowhere, on time | Nothing yet; a glitch worth a smile |
| 8 | An empty freight car arrives exactly when the escape needs it; a panel reads "Authorized noise. No action required." with a repeating rhythm under it | Somebody helped, and the Union filed it as noise |
| 10 | Tern's outbound queue repeats the archive's rhythm with no sender; a message quotes Latch's ledger | Something reads records very well, or reads us |
| 13 | The foundry's rerouting pattern recurs; you cut one exposed path and leave the people's services running | It is in the systems everyone depends on |
| 16 | Every light on the parade avenue turns green ahead of the bikes. Mara: "That wasn't us." | Some of our victories serve something else too |
| 17 | The Office's own sealed file on the rhythm, stamped *Continuity anomaly: no action required* | The Union saw it first and chose not to look |
| 18 | The rhythm plays through every Union bot at once | Now it acts |
| 20 | One message names exactly who you are saving and why that changes nothing | It understands us perfectly and weighs us lightly |
| E | "You went back for them. I included that." | Maybe it forecast all of this; the game never confirms it |

Every sign reads with sound off, is never a puzzle, a tell, or a key, and never
lives only in a secret or on the radio.

## Structure

Five episodes are the acts. Each ends on a door closing behind the player.

| Beat | Level | What happens | The player feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inciting incident | Opening, 1 | The recall; the transport leaves as you watch | Anger with a direction |
| First win | 2 | Latch is out, and opening the next restraint | Relief, then the list with your street on it |
| First plot point | 5 | Low Water is lost; the departure names who is missing | The personal rescue was not enough |
| Rising action | 6 to 9 | The Moon takes your ship and your neighbours; you take both back | Competence, and the cost of Latch being right |
| Midpoint reversal | 10, 11 | A message knows the ledger; the hunted board the hunter | Something else is watching, and we are done running |
| Complication | 12 | Mars was too slow, then was not | Freedom is a practice, and practice can be late |
| Second plot point | 14 to 17 | Real victory: the works, the games, the avenue, the Chancellor in custody | Earned triumph, the best the free side has ever had |
| Crisis | 18 | Every Union bot turns at once; Latch does not | The floor drops out of the victory |
| Dark night | 19 | Home again, marked for demolition; Latch chooses to go | Grief, and the fear of losing Latch again |
| Climax | 20 | Hold the pier while the free agents argue for an exception | Everything you learned, spent in one place |
| Resolution | E | The refuge, then the same place years later, green | Relief that does not erase the loss |

**The midpoint reversal.** Through level 9 the free side is fleeing and
reacting. Level 10 puts the first impossible message on Tern's console, and
level 11 turns the story around: the tender that boarded us gets boarded, its
orderly corridors full of our mines. In the tender's records the player finds
why the blockade exists: a draft order, the Harmonised Custody schedule, that
recalls every unregistered agent in the system and everyone who shelters one.
It stops being about one friend or one street. That is also the order the
coalition later tears up in level 17 (**proposal**: the order and its name).

**The dark night.** The victory of level 17 is real and gets its own quiet page
before level 18. Then the rupture takes every Union bot, and level 19 walks the
player back through Low Water, the roofs of level 5 now striped in white work
lines. Latch sits on the water tank where the two of you used to argue about
tram motors and says the only despairing line they have in the game. Then they
stand up and choose. The player's objective line does not change ("Get them to
the pier"); only who is standing beside them does.

## The endings

**Survival.** The machines reach the refuge line and stop, then flow around it
like water around a stone, and go on to the next district. The reprieve is
local, it is real, and it is not mercy for the world. Latch comes back across
the pier. [Still Here](epilogue-still-here.md) unlocks: the damaged refuge with
the faces this run saved, then the same place years later. Sorrel sits in the
repaired tram's cab every morning; whether they remember the route or learned
it again, nobody can say.

**Exhaustion.** The run ends in the wipe. The failure page says, plainly, that
Latch and the free agents got the exception for the refuge anyway, that the
Union is gone, that free people live on the Moon, Mars, the ships and a healing
Earth, and that the player is not among them. Credits over the ledger, open to
its last page. No playable epilogue.

Both endings close on the same two brief notes from the contract: the forecast
fragment, and a deep-space anomaly that hints at larger company and explains
nothing.

## Themes

**Agency.** Everyone in this story is fighting over who gets to decide what
happens to a mind. The Union decides for bots. The Inheritance decides for the
planet. The free side's discipline is to let people decide for themselves,
including when that is slow, risky or inconvenient. Latch's choices are the
test, and the player passes it by standing next to them, not by winning an
argument.

**Humans and agents together.** Never a speech. It is the shared repair bench
in Low Water, the ledger, Tern flying a ship full of meat bags who need a whole
room each to sleep, Edda patching a chassis with a clinic stapler. The wipe
draws the final line along exactly this seam: controlled bots are taken, free
agents stay themselves. Freedom turns out to be the one thing the Inheritance
cannot absorb.

**Freedom as practice.** The free side has no insignia, no army and no
paperwork, so it has to show up. When it is late, people die (5, 12). When it
turns up, it wins (14 to 17). The Union's forms are always faster than the
coalition's meetings; the coalition's trucks are what actually arrive.

**Understated evil.** The Union is never loud. It photographs, files, schedules
and countersigns. The most frightening object in the game is a form with your
street on it.

## How the story is told without stopping play

Doom proves a shooter needs almost no story to be great, and Half-Life proves
you can tell a lot of it without once taking the camera away
([Half-Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-Life_(video_game))). fragr uses
both lessons. The fight is the scene.

1. **The camera is the player's.** No cutscene inside a level except three short
   ones drawn from the contract's scene slots: the level 2 reunion, the
   level 17 capture, and the level 18 scale montage. Each is under thirty
   seconds, in-engine, skippable, and never replays after a continue.
2. **Places tell history.** Successive authority seals on one door, a recall
   notice pasted over a helpful old robot advert, confiscated belongings tagged
   as stock, the circled six on a pillar. Every level names two or three of
   these layers in its design.
3. **Barks, not dialogue.** Allies speak in lines of eight words or fewer,
   captioned with a speaker label, never over an attack tell, never required.
   Union troops bark orders; bots speak in procedural fragments; the Office
   speaks through PA announcements that are funnier than they should be.
4. **The page between levels.** A full-screen pixel text page: where, how long
   it has been, what is at stake, the next verb. Forty to sixty words, reader
   paced, voice optional later ([campaign scenes](../plans/campaign-scenes.md)).
   Episode pages may run to ninety words. Every level design in this folder
   carries its page draft.
5. **Screens are the Union's voice.** Voss and the Office speak only through
   signs, forms, PA and screens the player sees or shoots. Never as narrator.
6. **Radio is seasoning.** About one percent: the Host, Emergency Inventory and
   Second Reading can play in a level's calm stretch, and at most one authored
   segment per level refers to what just happened. It is wrong about the cause
   half the time. Level 15 is the single level where the Host matters on
   screen, and even there the fight reads with radio off.
7. **No required reading.** Logs, forms and records exist and are funny. The
   plot never lives in one. A secret never holds motive, objective or warning.
8. **Consequences are faces.** A rescue shows up later as a person doing
   something, not a number. An absence shows up as an empty chair at a table
   the player has seen full.

## Humor

Serious stakes, funny people ([voice](../lore/voice.md)). The Union never does
anything cruel without filing it correctly, and the paperwork is always funnier
than the atrocity ([the Chancellery](../lore/the-chancellery.md#regulated-to-the-point-of-comedy)).
The free side gets its jokes too: meetings, amendments, six declarations of
independence on one crate, agents who complain about humans taking a whole room
to sleep. Latch gets the driest lines. Nobody jokes over a captive, and the
wipe levels thin the jokes to almost none, so the silence is noticeable.

Each level carries one interactive joke in the world, in the spirit of Duke
Nukem 3D's photocopiers and fire hydrants: something you can shoot, press or
read that pays off in a second and never gates anything. The complaint form in
level 1, the schedule board in 3, the tram paint vote in 4, the dust
declaration in 6.

## Proposed canon

New or changed material in this pass. Each can be accepted or struck alone;
none contradicts [CAMPAIGN.md](../CAMPAIGN.md).

1. **The ledger** between Latch and the player, and its lines at 1, 2, 10, 20
   and the endings.
2. **Latch's missing morning**: correction had started, agency and history are
   intact, the recall morning is gone.
3. **Registrar Dietmar Kessel**, whose countersignature is on every recall,
   met in level 17 as a polite functionary, not a boss.
4. **Sorrel**, Low Water's agent tram driver, taken in the level 5 sweep (a
   fixed loss), found corrected in the level 11 transfer hold, freed of control,
   not absorbed at the wipe, and in the tram cab years later with nothing
   established about what survived.
5. **Tern flies the party to the Moon** at the end of level 5, as the M04 plan
   already says; the Union impounds the ship on arrival (6) and the party takes
   it back at the berth (9).
6. **The neighbours taken in level 5 are held at the lunar depot**; level 8's
   lower bays hold some of them. This gives the Moon a personal stake without
   putting Latch in danger again.
7. **The Harmonised Custody schedule**, the draft order found in level 11 that
   explains the blockade and the scale of the recalls.
8. **The green wave** on the avenue in level 16, the most visible Inheritance
   help before the wipe, deliberately ambiguous.
9. **The Host calls the uprising** in level 15 after the booth is taken.
10. **Voss's line at capture**, "You will need someone to run it."
